🇦🇷 Malvinas

Argentina took Malvinas (Falklands) oil to the Law of the Sea Tribunal — and London shielded its oil companies

Buenos Aires asked for urgent measures in Hamburg to stop Sea Lion, the first major oil project in Malvinas waters. The UK responded by invoking a law to protect the companies. What each side wants and what could happen.

J. Martineau 🇦🇷 Malvinas

Malvinas — GLOBALpatagonia Report

The fight over Malvinas (Falklands) oil has moved from diplomatic notes to international courts. On October 13, Argentina asked the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, in Hamburg, for provisional measures to stop Sea Lion, the first major oil project in the islands' waters. Almost simultaneously, London made clear it will protect the companies behind it.

What Argentina asked for

The request is part of the arbitration the government opened on September 28 under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. That day the United Kingdom was given two weeks to halt activity; the deadline expired on October 12 without a favourable answer. The filing relies on Article 290 of the Convention and makes four specific demands:

  • Sea Lion: that the UK prevent the start or continuation of hydrocarbon exploitation in the North Malvinas Basin.
  • Exploration data: that it not use information gathered during exploration to Argentina's detriment.
  • Marine environment: that it not hinder compliance with the Convention's environmental protection obligations.
  • New permits: that it refrain from measures that aggravate the dispute, in particular new exploitation authorisations.

It is important to understand the scope: filing the request does not suspend the project. The Tribunal must now decide whether to grant the measures and on what terms. In the ARA Libertad frigate case in 2012 it ruled in about a month, so a first decision could come in November, though no date is confirmed.

The Sea Lion platform, the project in the North Malvinas Basin that Argentina wants to stop. Photo: file.

London shields its companies

The British response was swift. The government rejected Argentina's move, argued that the activity is covered by licences issued by the islands' authorities — extended for five years in September — and announced it will use its Protection of Trading Interests legislation, a law that allows it to stop British companies from complying with other countries' extraterritorial sanctions or regulations.

In Parliament, Overseas Territories minister Uma Kumaran was blunt: the Argentine claim “does not invalidate the licences issued by the islands' government.” She warned that, if the situation continues, London could take further measures that, she said, “will be lawful, will be proportionate and will be forceful.”

The companies and the timeline

Sea Lion is operated by Israel's Navitas Petroleum (65%), with Britain's Rockhopper Exploration (35%) as partner; Rockhopper discovered the field in 2010. The plan calls for eleven subsea wells tied to a floating production unit. Development drilling is scheduled for 2027 and first commercial oil for the first quarter of 2028. On October 7, Rockhopper announced a US$44 million investment in its share of the company that owns that unit.

On the Argentine side, the pressure already has one concrete effect: according to the Presidency, Halliburton confirmed its subsidiaries will not provide services to the project. Navitas, for its part, acknowledged it is replacing two contractors, though it says it expects no material impact.

What is really at stake

The Law of the Sea Tribunal does not settle sovereignty disputes, and that is the main legal obstacle to Argentina's strategy: the Convention gives it no general jurisdiction over who owns a territory. That is why the filing avoids debating sovereignty and relies on concrete obligations over resources, the environment and the parties' conduct while the dispute remains open.

Even so, the move has clear political and economic value: it takes Sea Lion to an international forum, exposes the companies to reputational and financial risk, and forces London to show how much it is willing to spend to sustain the project. The Tribunal's decision, expected in the coming weeks, will set the course of the oil dispute in the South Atlantic.
